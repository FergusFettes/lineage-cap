The Head-Start Cap

If life usually gets there in the end, how far ahead of us could anyone have been in a sky this empty?

Fergus Fettes Read the note (PDF) Code and data doi:10.5281/zenodo.23406424

We are close to being able to spread beyond our own planet, and we see nobody else doing it. The usual explanation is that civilisations are rare: each planet has to win a chain of improbable transitions, the hard steps. Mills and colleagues have argued that Earth's record gives poor support for those improbable steps. The steps may simply have waited for conditions to change.

Take that seriously and a problem appears. If lineages usually get there in the end, older ones had a head start, and some of them should be visible by now. This page lets you run the note's calculation yourself. Two clocks are compared. In one, every planet starts from scratch at its own birth. In the other, progress runs from a shared cosmic start and carries between environments.

Flat space, galaxies scattered at random, perfect detection of any galaxy a loud lineage has crossed. The sky counts as empty when that has at least the chance you set below (5% in the note). Every number is an edge for these inputs, not a probability about the real world.

Real lineages will not all take the same time. Give each one its own duration, drawn independently from a normal distribution, all running from one shared start. The fastest of ten billion draws lands far ahead of the mean, so the sky forces the mean emergence into the future. We end up sitting in a thin leading edge: the first flush.

These figures depend on the far left tail of the duration law, near the 10⁻¹² percentile, where nothing physical pins the shape. A normal tail always allows a few much faster lineages. A hard minimum duration would allow none. Read this as arithmetic for one tail shape.

What the calculation assumes

  • Trajectories, not planets. N counts effective retained-progress trajectories per galaxy in Earth-equivalent units: 10¹¹ stars × 0.1. It grows linearly from cosmic age 0.2 Gyr to today, 13.8 Gyr. Trajectories are never destroyed.
  • Turning loud. A lineage that crosses the threshold turns loud with probability q. It then spreads through its galaxy (30 kpc) and keeps spreading at speed v. The note's results mostly depend on Λ = qN.
  • Seeing it, or meeting it. A galaxy is visible once its front has crossed it and the light has reached us. Arrival means the front itself reaching us. Each galaxy is independent, so P(empty) = e−μ.
  • Geometry. Space is flat with a fixed galaxy density. That is fair for the few megaparsecs behind the 14 Myr cap. Over the gigaparsecs the planet clock integrates, treat it as a scaling statement.
  • What it does not do. It does not derive the shared clock from chemistry, model survey completeness, or turn percentiles into odds for our own existence.
